# Drones map suspicion, not mines: lessons for MineGuard

Drone surveys are now a real part of humanitarian mine action. In practice they are tools for **non-technical survey (NTS) and prioritisation**: they find surface items, conflict features and likely hazard boundaries. They do not find buried mines reliably, and nobody treats them as a basis for declaring land safe. The best independent multi-system trial, run by UNDP in Ukraine in 2024, found that the strongest teams located about 55–78% of targets depending on category. Only 60% of buried objects were found by any team, and buried plastic items "remained unreliable" for every technology ([JCWD 28.3](https://www.jmu.edu/news/cisr/2024/10/28-3/08-283-crowther.shtml)). Terrain data (LiDAR DTMs, DEM differencing, micro-relief) adds real value as **indirect evidence**. It reveals trenches, foxholes, craters and access routes under vegetation, and in principle it can show where floods or landslides moved soil. No source shows LiDAR or DEM relief detecting an individual buried mine ([JMU CISR 27.2](https://www.jmu.edu/news/cisr/2023/06/272/07-272-james.shtml)). The International Mine Action Standards (IMAS) make this distinction binding. Indirect evidence can only create a *suspected* hazardous area, and "any decisions relating to contamination shall be based on evidence, not the lack thereof" ([IMAS 07.11](https://www.mineactionstandards.org/standards/07-11/)). For MineGuard V2.2, the most credible stance is therefore not to improve its detection numbers. It is to present its simulated pipeline as an honest concept demonstrator. That means its null detector result (0 of 12) is reported with proper statistics. Its cut/fill map is reframed as a relief-anomaly layer for survey prioritisation. Every mark on real campus imagery is visibly synthetic, labelled "suspected", and impossible to read as a statement about the real ground.

## Surface mines are tractable from the air; buried low-metal mines are not

The evidence divides sharply by target type. For **surface-laid** mines, mostly the PFM-1 "butterfly" mine, RGB and thermal imaging with deep learning give strong results on test plots. They are fragile under domain shift, though. Binghamton's Faster R-CNN reached **99.3% accuracy on a partially withheld test set but only 71.5% on a completely withheld one** in the same paper ([Baur et al. 2020](https://mdpi.com/2072-4292/12/5/859)). The 71.5% is the honest generalisation figure. The SULAND v2 benchmark shows the same pattern: best in-distribution mAP@50 was 0.908, against **0.799 out of distribution with recall of 0.675**, and the authors state that high in-distribution accuracy does not mean operational readiness ([arXiv 2607.28996](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28996)). Target size matters more than any architecture choice. In George Mason's RGB+LWIR fusion study, the best YOLOv11 configuration reached 86.8% mAP. Across conditions, though, accuracy was **61.9% for anti-tank mines and only 19.2% for anti-personnel mines**. Mean mAP fell from 65.1% at 5 m altitude to 25.4% at 20 m ([Gallagher et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.20487v1)). An RGB YOLOv11 study found precision of 74–80% on 3D-printed replica mines but **recall of just 14–24%** ([MDPI 2673-7418/6/3/54](https://www.mdpi.com/2673-7418/6/3/54)). A hyperspectral PFM-1 benchmark showed why ROC-AUC flatters detectors on sparse targets. Full-region average precision was 0.043–0.226 even though AUC stayed at or above 0.994, and only two targets were in the test set ([arXiv 2602.10434](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.10434v2)).

**Thermal infrared** is the only passive sensor with field proof against *buried* mines, and that proof is narrow. Over legacy minefields in Chad, a small drone's LWIR camera at 35 m AGL showed thermal anomalies from **27 buried legacy landmines in a single image**. Validation came from flying beside rows of visible surface mines, and the setting was dry desert ([JMU CISR, Fardoulis 2021](https://www.jmu.edu/news/cisr/2021/07/15-242-fardoulis.shtml)). The physics depends on timing. Buried objects change how the ground stores and conducts heat, so contrast appears and disappears through the diurnal cycle ([Fardoulis 2022](https://www.jmu.edu/news/cisr/2022/06/253/12-253-fardoulis.shtml)). Studies disagree on the best hour: morning for the PFM-1 ([de Smet & Nikulin 2018](https://mdpi.com/2072-4292/10/11/1672)), evening in one study reporting "nearly 88%" accuracy ([arXiv 2410.23998](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.23998)), and night-time in an ISPRS study ([ISPRS 2018](https://isprs-archives.copernicus.org/articles/XLII-1/413/2018/isprs-archives-XLII-1-413-2018.pdf)). That disagreement is itself the finding. Thermal detectability depends on site, soil and depth, and no universal rule exists.

**Magnetometers and GPR** are the subsurface hopes, and both remain mostly at demonstration stage. Drone magnetometry works for ferrous UXO when the sensor is flown very low with tight lines. A Spanish trial recorded **2–18 nT anomalies over eleven buried UXOs** at 2–7 m AGL with 1 m line spacing ([MDPI Drones 10(2):88](https://www.mdpi.com/2504-446X/10/2/88)). Trials over BM-21 rockets in Ukraine needed 3 m line spacing for consistent anomalies ([CISR 24.1](https://issuu.com/cisr-journal/docs/24.1-all/s/10723813)). Ukraine's Ministry of Defence now flies magnetometer drones for NTS and claims more than 10 ha per day, but publishes no detection or false-alarm figures ([MoD Ukraine](https://mod.gov.ua/en/news/detection-of-explosives-including-underground-threats-unmanned-aerial-systems-with-magnetometers-are-being-deployed-for-demining-in-the-mykolaiv-region)). Magnetometry is physically blind to minimum-metal plastic AP mines. UAV GPR-SAR can image both metallic and dielectric targets. It needs **centimetre-level positioning** ([García-Fernández et al.](https://digibuo.uniovi.es/dspace/bitstream/10651/51029/2/SistemadeGPR.pdf)), its best field work imaged targets buried in sand, and its authors frame that as showing "potential" ([Bähnemann et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.10108)). GICHD's equipment catalogue still lists drone-mounted metal detectors and GPR as research-stage ([GICHD](https://gichd.org/publications-resources/equipment-catalogue/unmanned-systems)). Baur's doctoral comparison concluded that UAV RGB was the most cost-effective way to find *surface* targets, while the handheld EMI detector remained most effective for *subsurface* ones ([Columbia](https://academiccommons.columbia.edu/doi/10.7916/fba0-sw83)).

| Sensor | Best evidence | Key numbers | Hard limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| RGB + deep learning | Many test-plot studies (PFM-1, PMA-2) | 99.3% → 71.5% on held-out site; OOD recall 0.675 | Surface only; vegetation; domain shift |
| Thermal LWIR | Chad legacy minefield; GMU fusion | 27 buried mines visible in one image; AP 19.2% vs AT 61.9% | Diurnal timing, soil, season, vegetation |
| LiDAR | HALO Angola trial | Trenches, foxholes 0.6–0.8 m deep mapped under canopy | Sees geometry, not mines |
| Magnetometer | UXO trials, Ukraine MoD | 2–18 nT anomalies at 2–7 m AGL | Ferrous only; needs ~1–3 m sensor height |
| GPR-SAR | Lab and sand-pit demos | No field Pd/FAR found | cm positioning; research stage |

Fusion is the obvious next step, and almost nobody has measured it. The clearest figure is the GMU result: **10–30% thermal blended into RGB at 5–10 m altitude** was optimal, and multi-season training added 1.8–9.6% ([Gallagher et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.20487v1)). Bistatic GPR gains were shown in simulation only ([DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/2fcf06713c7347f394a7e54c26ffd3aa)). A nine-modality campaign at RIT has collected 34 co-registered datasets but has published no fusion results ([RIT DIRS](https://www.rit.edu/dirs/research/advancing-operational-landmine-detection-through-uav-based-multi-sensor-sensing-and)). The UNDP trial's own conclusion was that gains will come more from fusion and AI than from new hardware, and that human experts must still review the data ([JCWD 28.3](https://www.jmu.edu/news/cisr/2024/10/28-3/08-283-crowther.shtml)).

## Terrain data finds the war around the mines, not the mines themselves

The strongest case for drone LiDAR in mine action comes from HALO Trust and Routescene at Cuito Cuanavale, Angola. A 16-channel LiDAR on a DJI M600 Pro, flown at 40–50 m AGL, produced bare-earth DTMs through dense vegetation. Satellite, RGB and thermal imagery could not see the features those DTMs revealed. At one defensive mine line the LiDAR mapped **about 500 m of main trench, 281 m of communication trench, 34 foxholes averaging 0.67 m deep and 2.54 m wide, and craters about 5.85 m wide**. It also revealed historical tracks that could serve as safe access routes ([LiDAR Magazine 2023](https://lidarmag.com/2023/10/07/uav-lidar-improves-landmine-clearance-planning/)). HALO used these outputs to target technical survey and clearance. The authors are explicit that the LiDAR **did not detect mines directly**: craters were used to *infer* where mine lines lay. They also list hard practical limits: flight range of about 1–1.5 km against minefields 20–30 km long, two flights a day, and GNSS post-processing that arrived only about 24 hours later ([JMU CISR 27.2](https://www.jmu.edu/news/cisr/2023/06/272/07-272-james.shtml)). Conflict archaeology confirms how reliably LiDAR picks out features at this scale. Over 12% of a Flanders study area carries surface traces of the First World War ([Gheyle et al. 2018](https://forskning.ruc.dk/da/publications/scratching-the-surface-of-war-airborne-laser-scans-of-the-great-w/)), and drone LiDAR with SLAM found more than 940 Battle of the Bulge features in forested Ardennes ([Antiquity 2023](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/lidar-and-conflict-archaeology-the-battle-of-the-bulge-19441945/5050B5FFFAA961C3F75FF7FBC4FE1F4E)).

The feature scale sets the resolution requirement. Trenches and large craters show at roughly 0.5–1 m DTM resolution. Foxholes and anti-vehicle mine craters, 2–6 m wide and 0.5–0.8 m deep, need sub-metre sampling and dense ground returns. An individual AP mine is 6–12 cm across and usually flush or shallow. Its relief signature sits at or below the vertical noise of drone LiDAR and photogrammetry, especially after decades of weathering, and no source shows micro-topography detecting one. Disturbed-soil cues over freshly buried objects do exist. They fade over time, and many unrelated activities disturb soil in the same way ([DTIC AD1018426](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/AD1018426.pdf)). The UNDP trial found the reverse problem: items buried the day before were *easier* to spot because of fresh disturbance, so the organisers recommend burying test items at least a week, ideally a month, in advance ([JCWD 28.3](https://www.jmu.edu/news/cisr/2024/10/28-3/08-283-crowther.shtml)).

**Mine migration** is where terrain analysis has the clearest unmet need. During the May 2014 Balkan floods, Bosnia's mine action centre warned that landslides had moved whole minefields, markings included, and that many of the country's more than 100,000 remaining mines could have been dislodged ([NPR 2014](https://www.npr.org/sections/thetwo-way/2014/05/20/314379214/balkan-floods-expose-deadly-mines-from-1990s-civil-war)). Baselt et al. argue that floods, mass movements and erosion carry mines into previously uncontaminated land. They say the problem can "only be tackled using a stochastic-deterministic model" that produces risk-hazard maps, but that model remains a proposal that needs laboratory and field work ([Baselt et al. 2023](https://doaj.org/article/cd6d828e969b4d55afae87b6d836a970)). This is the most legitimate home for cut/fill-style analysis. Differencing pre- and post-event DEMs would delineate erosion source zones (cut) and deposition zones (fill), flagging where mines may have left or arrived. Hazard polygons could then be updated and new NTS triggered. No mine-action organisation was found doing this operationally.

Cut/fill analysis therefore has defensible and indefensible uses. It is defensible as before/after change detection, and as residual relief against a *local, detrended* reference surface. That second approach highlights pits, trenches, berms and spoil, much as the openness and sky-view-factor visualisations used in WWI archaeology do ([Univ. Bergamo](https://aisberg.unibg.it/retrieve/c28de8c8-4ce7-46a8-8dc0-3a0b376d426e/1395-Article%20Text-10660-1-10-20230502%20%283%29.pdf)). Cut/fill is also defensible for QA of mechanical clearance depth and for slope and access planning. It is indefensible as a mine detector, as a hazard score, or as grounds for releasing land. Comparing natural sloping terrain against an arbitrary flat plane is also meaningless, because the resulting "depths" mostly reflect topography rather than disturbance.

Under IMAS, all of this is **indirect evidence feeding NTS and technical survey planning**. The current standards define a Suspected Hazardous Area (SHA) as an area with "reasonable suspicion of EO contamination on the basis of indirect evidence". A Confirmed Hazardous Area (CHA) requires direct evidence, and land release demands "all reasonable effort" through NTS, technical survey and/or clearance ([IMAS 07.11](https://www.mineactionstandards.org/standards/07-11/)). No IMAS title is devoted to drones or remote sensing ([IMAS list](https://www.mineactionstandards.org/standards/)). GICHD frames remote sensing, sensor fusion and AI as tools that "can significantly enhance" survey, not replace it ([GICHD Land Release Agenda](https://www.gichd.org/fileadmin/uploads/gichd/Media/GICHD-resources/Innovation_Hub/Land_Release_Agenda_v10_WEB.pdf)). Ukraine went furthest when its national land-release standard SSU 8820-5:2024 recognised "UAV and satellite imagery analysis" as a survey method, in alignment with IMAS ([MoD Ukraine](https://mod.gov.ua/en/news/state-standardization-now-recognizes-the-use-of-uav-and-satellite-imagery-in-demining-activities)).

## Ukraine runs on AI-flagged RGB imagery, but public data stays thin

The systems actually deployed in 2024–2026 are overwhelmingly **EO/RGB drone mapping with AI or human image review**. Safe Pro's SpotlightAI has been used by NPA in Ukraine since November 2023, and IEEE Spectrum reports HALO also uses it ([NPA](https://www.npaid.org/mine-action-and-disarmament/news/npa-ukraine-conducts-pilot-project-with-safe-pro-ai-for-automated-processing-of-drone-imagery); [IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/ukraine-drones)). AWS has funded HALO with $4M to train models on 542 drone flights of minefield imagery. Those models need ongoing vetting by HALO staff to remove false positives and negatives ([HALO](https://www.halotrust.org/news/artificial-intelligence-to-help-accelerate-landmine-clearance/)). Headline productivity claims should be read sceptically. An 18-month study co-presented by NPA and the vendor's co-founder at GICHD's 2025 conference claimed **800+% NTS productivity** and about 50% lower labour cost per hectare. The press release calls this "independent research", yet gives no precision, recall or false-alarm figures ([Safe Pro, Nov 2025](https://safeprogroup.com/safe-pros-artificial-intelligence-delivers-800-productivity-surge-in-ukraine-demining-surveys-according-to-independent-research-presented-at-leading-global-conference/)). No deployed AI system publishes probability of detection or false-alarm rate. The anonymised UNDP numbers remain the only independent comparison. In those results, Organisation 7 (EO plus magnetometer) found 71% of metal and 67% of buried objects in the CHA, and Organisation 4 (EO only) found 78% of surface items. Wind, flight bans and faults stopped four of seven teams from surveying the SHA at all ([JCWD 28.3](https://www.jmu.edu/news/cisr/2024/10/28-3/08-283-crowther.shtml)).

Public datasets are small, mostly surface, and all use inert or replica items. **AMLID** is the best openly licensed UAS set: 12,078 labelled RGB+LWIR images of 21 mine types at four altitudes, two seasons and three lighting conditions, under CC BY 4.0 ([Zenodo](https://zenodo.org/records/18001447)). **SULAND v2** has 33,771 RGB images of PFM-1 and PMA-2 with explicit out-of-distribution splits ([arXiv 2607.28996](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28996)). Universidad del Valle's thermal set is the only open aerial set with **controlled burial depths** (0, 1, 5 and 10 cm, with a mine-free control zone). Its labels are weak, image-level ones with no bounding boxes ([Mendeley](https://doi.org/10.17632/732ngnf4r3.4)). MineInsight adds LiDAR and multispectral data from a ground robot ([arXiv 2506.04842](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.04842v2)). The Demining Research Community's seeded field documents its flight parameters ([Zenodo 15324498](https://zenodo.org/records/15324498)). There is **no public real-data GPR landmine dataset**: the HoloMine authors state that "there is currently no common dataset available" ([arXiv 2502.21054](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.21054v1)). No public drone magnetometer landmine dataset was found either.

## Mission parameters follow from pixels on target, not from altitude rules

Published optical successes on AP-sized targets cluster tightly. **Binghamton flew 10 m AGL at 2 m/s with 80% overlap** over 10 × 20 m grids, at 3.5 minutes per grid ([Baur et al. 2020](https://mdpi.com/2072-4292/12/5/859)). The Demining Research Community flew 12 m with 80% frontlap and 75% sidelap, producing RGB at 0.21 cm/px and thermal at 0.37 cm/px ([Zenodo 15324498](https://zenodo.org/records/15324498)). The GMU collapse at 10–20 m used a 1080p wide-angle camera. That points to **ground sampling distance and pixels on target as the controlling variable**, not altitude itself ([Gallagher et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.20487v1)). GSD ≈ altitude × pixel pitch ÷ focal length. A 6–12 cm mine at 0.2–0.4 cm/px spans roughly 25–50 pixels, so any altitude limit should be derived from the camera, not copied from a paper.

Magnetometry inverts this logic: get the sensor as low as possible. Industry guidance recommends about 1–2 m sensor height, 0.5 m lines for grenades and submunitions, 1 m for shells, about 2 m/s, a downward rangefinder for terrain following, and wind below 5–8 m/s ([SPH Engineering](https://www.sphengineering.com/news/drone-uxo-detection-magnetometer-surveys)). A patent describes a "3-3-3" rule (3 m height, 3 m spacing, 3 m/s) for large rockets ([US12055392B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US12055392)). UAV GPR works at about 0.5 m standoff ([PMC7218851](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7218851/)). That is why terrain-following over a DEM matters far more for subsurface sensors than for cameras. No mine-action document describing DEM-based terrain following was found, although it is standard UAV practice.

Positioning in research trials relies on GNSS-surveyed ground control points (GCPs) and re-optimisation, giving "cm accurate coordinates" ([Baur et al. 2020](https://mdpi.com/2072-4292/12/5/859)). A low-cost RTK drone without GCPs did not achieve acceptable accuracy ([PMC8200111](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8200111/)). No IMAS figure for drone detection handover accuracy was found. IMAS 05.10 does require geospatial accuracy to be "well defined and documented" ([IMAS 05.10](https://www.mineactionstandards.org/standards/05-10/)). Confidence is reported as mAP or accuracy, often with very large spread (86.8% ± 30.5% in the GMU study). No source describes calibrated per-detection probabilities handed to clearance teams ([Gallagher et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.20487v1)). A defensible output would therefore carry, for each mark, the score, the contributing sensors, the GSD and AGL, the flight time and a positional uncertainty. It would also include a coverage layer showing where detection was unreliable.

## Prioritised recommendations for MineGuard V2.2

MineGuard's constraints are its strengths if it states them plainly. IMAS 03.40 treats trials as a way to *find problems*, and asks for results that are statistically sufficient "to ensure that the results have not arisen from chance". Its lowest trial category is a concept or technology demonstrator ([IMAS 03.40](https://www.mineactionstandards.org/standards/03-40/)). That category is MineGuard's honest ceiling. The recommendations below are ordered by how much credibility each buys per hour of work.

**First, report the frozen detector's 0/12 as a properly bounded null result, and stop at that.** With zero detections out of 12 targets, an exact Clopper-Pearson calculation (standard statistics, not a mine-action clause) puts the 95% two-sided upper bound on PD at about **26%** (one-sided about 22%). The correct sentence is therefore "PD = 0/12 (95% CI 0–26%) on the sealed V2.2 synthetic set". Report false alarms per hectare with a Poisson interval alongside. Do not retune the frozen detector against the same 12 targets. Binomial reasoning cuts both ways ([Sci. Rep. 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1038/s41598-021-03755-1)): even a future 30/30 would support only PD ≥ about 90% at 95% confidence. A diagnosis of *why* it found nothing is more valuable than any number. Likely causes include GSD versus synthetic target size, thermal contrast, threshold choice, and train/test generator mismatch. Report that diagnosis per condition stratum, as both IMAS 03.40 and the simulation literature favour ([arXiv 2608.09691](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.09691)).

**Second, make every output speak IMAS.** Each map, CSV, figure and app screen should say "SIMULATION – suspected anomaly (indirect evidence only) – not for operational use". Areas with no marks should read "no suspected anomaly found in simulation", never "clear", "safe" or "low risk", because IMAS 07.11 forbids decisions based on the absence of evidence ([IMAS 07.11](https://www.mineactionstandards.org/standards/07-11/)). A blank map read as "no hazard" is the costliest failure mode in this field.

**Third, reframe the cut/fill map as a relief-anomaly layer.** Swap any flat reference plane for a detrended or smoothed local surface, or for a simulated "before" DEM. Label positive and negative residuals "surface relief anomaly – survey prioritisation only". Size the synthetic features on HALO's measured foxholes and craters (0.6–0.8 m deep, 2.5–6 m wide) ([LiDAR Magazine](https://lidarmag.com/2023/10/07/uav-lidar-improves-landmine-clearance-planning/)), not on mine-sized bumps. A strong, honest demo would be a simulated flood or landslide DEM difference that flags where mines *could* have migrated, citing the 2014 Bosnia case and Baselt et al.

**Fourth, rebuild the synthetic survey around evidence-based parameters and a sealed virtual test lane.** Derive altitude from the simulated camera's GSD, targeting about 25+ pixels across the target. Fly about 2 m/s with 75–80% overlap for RGB and thermal. Model magnetometer and GPR passes as low, terrain-following lines driven by the simulated LiDAR DEM. Pre-register a fixed seed, target list, burial depths and clutter (fragments, rocks, vegetation, soil moisture, thermal time of day), hash it, commit it and score it once. Include a whole held-out "site" to expose domain shift, the effect SULAND v2 and Baur et al. both measured. Optionally, validate the RGB/thermal branch against AMLID (CC BY 4.0) to show what real-data domain gap looks like ([Zenodo](https://zenodo.org/records/18001447)). Report a PD-versus-FAR curve, not a single accuracy figure, and attach score, sensor, GSD, AGL and position uncertainty to each mark.

**Fifth, handle the BITM campus imagery so it can never be read as a hazard claim.** The campus is a real, uncontaminated place, so a red mark on its satellite view risks misleading a viewer or alarming a reader. Keep the "VIRTUAL route" label. Render synthetic targets and suspected-anomaly marks in a visibly non-operational style, such as hollow outlined symbols with a persistent "SYNTHETIC TARGET – NOT A REAL OBJECT" legend. Watermark the map layer. Never export mark coordinates as if they were field findings. Do not publish imagery showing identifiable people, following the Humanitarian UAV Code's data guidance ([SciDev.Net](https://www.scidev.net/global/supported-content/do-no-harm-a-code-to-guide-use-of-humanitarian-drones/)). Never use real minefield coordinates; under IMAS 05.10 those belong to national authorities and are shared only with authorised parties ([IMAS 05.10](https://www.mineactionstandards.org/standards/05-10/)). Show the dashed simulated survey area and a coverage-quality layer, so viewers see what was "surveyed" and where the simulated sensor was blind.

**Sixth, state the scope claim and the sensor-physics limits in the README and report.** MineGuard is a concept demonstrator. Its LiDAR maps relief, not mines. Its simulated thermal is uncalibrated against real sensors. Magnetometry cannot see plastic mines. No real-world validation was done, and any real use would require independent T&E under IMAS protocols and national accreditation.

## Conclusion

The literature changes what "good" looks like for a project like MineGuard. Even the best-funded real systems publish no field detection or false-alarm rates. The one independent trial shows buried plastic mines defeating every aerial technology. The field's quiet consensus is that drones are evidence-gathering instruments for deciding *where to look*, with humans and ground clearance deciding *what is there*. A student simulation cannot compete on detection, but it can lead on something the sector visibly lacks: transparent metrics with confidence bounds, pre-registered test sets, honest null results, and outputs whose wording maps exactly onto IMAS evidence categories. MineGuard's 0/12 result, properly bounded and diagnosed, is more aligned with that standard than a vendor's unexplained 800%.

The most underexploited idea in the sources is terrain change. Floods and landslides demonstrably move mines beyond recorded hazard polygons. Proposed models exist but are unvalidated, and DEM differencing is exactly the cut/fill machinery MineGuard has already built. Repositioning that map from "where mines are" to "where the ground moved, and where suspicion should therefore move with it" turns MineGuard's weakest claim into its most defensible contribution.
