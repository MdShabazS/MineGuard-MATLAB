## 1. What test and evaluation protocols and metrics (PD, FAR, ROC, confidence) are standard for mine detection systems?

### Takeaway
IMAS 03.40 asks for independent, statistically sufficient trials that are carefully designed and reported in a standard format. The methods themselves now sit in IMAS Test and Evaluation Protocols (T&EPs), which include the former CEN Workshop Agreements such as CWA 14747 (metal detectors). Detection performance is treated as a binomial problem. Because PD is usually high and the number of targets small, results should be reported with exact (Clopper-Pearson) confidence bounds, not as a single point value.

### Cited Findings
- IMAS 03.40, titled "Test and evaluation of mine action equipment", is Edition 1, Amendment 6, latest publication 1 Jun 2013. Its stated purpose is to "provide an independent assessment of the suitability and effectiveness of mine action equipment". Results feed planning, procurement and Quality Assurance. — [IMAS 03.40 (mineactionstandards.org)](https://www.mineactionstandards.org/standards/03-40/)
- IMAS 03.40 says a trial's "principal purpose" is to provide quantitative data. The data must be sufficient "in statistical terms to ensure that the results have not arisen from chance". For trials with several variables it advises consulting a statistician experienced in equipment trials. — [IMAS 03.40](https://www.mineactionstandards.org/standards/03-40/)
- IMAS 03.40 builds independence through a Sponsor / Organiser / Supervisor structure and recommends accredited test facilities for accuracy, repeatability and replicability. Trial categories are concept and technology demonstrator, development, acceptance, and consumer reports. Consumer reports must rest on "a rigorous (and defensible) evaluation methodology". The final report follows the format in Annex C. — [IMAS 03.40](https://www.mineactionstandards.org/standards/03-40/)
- IMAS 03.40 frames trials as a way to find problems early, not only to confirm that equipment works. Trial design should allow for unexpected results and equipment failure. — [IMAS 03.40](https://www.mineactionstandards.org/standards/03-40/)
- The ITEP programme (International Test and Evaluation Program for Humanitarian Demining) closed in July 2010. The related CEN Workshop Agreements are now maintained within the IMAS system. — [IMAS 03.40](https://www.mineactionstandards.org/standards/03-40/)
- IMAS now uses T&EPs for test methodology. These include the former CWAs, with metal detectors, machines and PPE given as examples. (Snippet-level.) — [IMAS 09.30 copy (Scribd)](https://es.scribd.com/document/375632874/IMAS-09-30-T-EP-EOD-Competency-Standards-09-30-01-2014-Version-1-0); a 2023 overview of IMAS revisions mentions a new T&EP and an updated Technical Note — [JMU CISR Journal, 2023](https://jmu.edu/news/cisr/2023/06/272/02-272-logie.shtml)
- CWA 14747-1:2003, titled "Humanitarian mine action. Test and evaluation. Metal detectors", was published by CEN on 1 June 2003 and is listed by BSI as "Current" (ICS 95.040, descriptor "performance testing"). — [BSI Knowledge](https://knowledge.bsigroup.com/products/humanitarian-mine-action-test-and-evaluation-metal-detectors). The EVS (Estonian) catalogue reportedly lists Part 1 as withdrawn from 02.03.2016 (snippet-level), so its status is disputed. — [EVS](https://www.evs.ee/en/cwa-14747-1-2003)
- CWA 14747-2:2008, "Humanitarian mine action — Test and evaluation — Soil characterization for metal detector and ground penetrating radar performance", covers soil effects. Its contents list fixed-depth and equivalent-detection-depth tests (snippet-level). — [BSI listing](https://knowledge.bsigroup.com/products/humanitarian-mine-action-test-and-evaluation-soil-characterization-for-metal-detector-and-ground-penetrating-radar-performance); [CWA 14747-2 PDF (Univ. Florence repository)](https://flore.unifi.it/bitstream/2158/673775/1/CWA-14747-2-Soil.pdf)
- CWA 15044:2004, "Test and evaluation of demining machines", is the companion CWA for machines. — [CWA 15044 PDF (MSB Turkey archive)](https://www.msb.gov.tr/Content/Upload/Docs/mafam/CWA/CWA_15044_2004_TE_of_Demining_Machines.pdf)
- Binomial framing (snippet-level): a Ground Penetrating Radar textbook notes that if 10 mines are tested and all are detected (PD = 100%), confidence in that claim is still limited by the sample size, and the bounds come from the binomial distribution. — [Ground Penetrating Radar, 2nd ed., §12.3 (GlobalSpec)](https://www.globalspec.com/reference/29024/203279/12-3-performance-and-test-assessment)
- A Scientific Reports (2021) paper on explosives detectors assesses binary trials with binomial statistics following Clopper and Pearson. When testing against a requirement it recommends a one-sided bound, and it stresses exact binomial methods when PD is high and samples are small (snippet-level). — [Sci. Rep. 2021, s41598-021-03755-1](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1038/s41598-021-03755-1)
- NIST work on pass-fail testing covers both probability of detection and probability of false alarm at a stated confidence level. It defines a false-alarm critical value from the upper binomial confidence bound (snippet-level). — [NIST J. Res. 114(3)](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/jres/114/3/V114.N03.A05.pdf); [PMC4646571](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4646571/)
- A 2010 draft from the ITEP Secretariat on mechanical demining equipment covers confidence-interval calculation. It expresses performance as the percentage of AP mines neutralised in a test lane (snippet-level). — [Lessons Learned T&E Demining Machine Part 4 (MSB)](https://msb.gov.tr/Content/Upload/Docs/mafam/CWA/Lessons_Learned_TE_Demining_Machine_Part_4.pdf)

### Inferences
- For MineGuard, the closest analogue to a standard T&E is a sealed "virtual test lane": a fixed, pre-registered set of simulated targets and clutter, scored once. Report PD with an exact Clopper-Pearson 95% interval, and report false alarms per unit area (for example per m² or per hectare) with a Poisson or binomial interval. Add a ROC or PD-vs-FAR curve over the detector threshold.
- Rule-of-three sanity check (standard statistics, not taken from these sources): with n targets and zero misses, the one-sided 95% upper bound on the miss rate is about 3/n. So 30 targets all detected only supports PD ≥ about 90% at 95% confidence. The writer should present this as general statistics, not a mine-action clause.
- IMAS 03.40's categories suggest MineGuard should call itself at most a "concept / technology demonstrator" simulation, not an acceptance trial or a consumer report.

## 2. What language and safeguards do standards require when presenting detection results or maps?

### Takeaway
Under IMAS 07.11, any decision about contamination must rest on evidence, not on the lack of it. Indirect evidence makes an area only a Suspected Hazardous Area. An area is confirmed (CHA) only on direct evidence, and land counts as cleared only once hazards have been removed to a specified depth and checked through QA and QC. A remote-sensing or simulated output is therefore at best indirect evidence: a "suspected" area or anomaly that needs technical survey or clearance. It can never be presented as "safe" or "cleared". Maps and data are owned and controlled by the national authority (NMAA) under information-security and sharing policies.

### Cited Findings
- IMAS 07.11 "Land release" is Edition 2, latest publication 16 Jan 2026. §3.19 defines a Suspected Hazardous Area as an "area where there is reasonable suspicion of EO contamination on the basis of indirect evidence". §3.4 defines a Confirmed Hazardous Area as an "area where the presence of EO contamination has been confirmed on the basis of direct evidence". — [IMAS 07.11](https://www.mineactionstandards.org/standards/07-11/)
- IMAS 07.11 §4.2: "Any decisions relating to contamination shall be based on evidence, not the lack thereof." The standard also says fear of explosive ordnance alone does not count as evidence. — [IMAS 07.11](https://www.mineactionstandards.org/standards/07-11/)
- IMAS 07.11 distinguishes non-technical survey (data collection and analysis "without intrusive interventions") from technical survey (with intrusive interventions). §3.12 defines land release as applying "all reasonable effort" through NTS, TS and/or clearance. §3.1 calls "all reasonable effort" the "minimum acceptable level of effort", with the NMAA deciding when further effort becomes unreasonable. — [IMAS 07.11](https://www.mineactionstandards.org/standards/07-11/)
- IMAS 07.11 §5: land should be accepted only once "a sufficiently high level of confidence" has been reached. §4.3: the same confidence is required whether land is cancelled, reduced or cleared. Information must be reliable and well documented before land is handed over. — [IMAS 07.11](https://www.mineactionstandards.org/standards/07-11/)
- IMAS 07.11 §3.3 defines cleared land as an area where "all specified EO hazards have been removed and/or destroyed to a specified clearance depth". — [IMAS 07.11](https://www.mineactionstandards.org/standards/07-11/)
- IMAS 09.10 "Clearance requirements" is Edition 2, Amendment 6 (2020). Land counts as "cleared" when all mine and ERW hazards have been removed or destroyed from the specified area to the specified depth. Without reliable depth information, for metal-detector clearance of minimum-metal mines "the default depth should not be less than 130mm". Quality is managed in two stages: QA (accreditation and monitoring, with procedures that are "transparent and auditable") and QC (inspection of cleared land before release). — [IMAS 09.10](https://www.mineactionstandards.org/standards/09-10/)
- GICHD land-release guidance describes technical survey as investigating CHAs "to gain evidence on the presence or absence of mines/ERW" (snippet-level). — [GICHD, Land Release Technical Methods, Apr 2011](https://gichd.org/fileadmin/uploads/gichd/Publications/Land-Release-Tech-Methods-Apr2011.pdf)
- IMAS 05.10 "Information management for mine action" is Edition 2, Amendment 2, latest publication 6 Mar 2023. §5.1.1: the NMAA "holds final ownership of any data or information collected during the course of mine action operations". §5.4 requires an information storage and sharing policy, including "Ethical rules and considerations for data sharing", and an information security policy signed by all users. §7.2: data "is protected from unauthorised access and shared only with authorised parties", and personal information is managed to protect beneficiaries' privacy, with consent. §8.2: geospatial accuracy must be "well defined and documented". — [IMAS 05.10](https://www.mineactionstandards.org/standards/05-10/)
- IMAS 05.10 has no specific rules on the sensitivity of location data, data classification levels or encryption (based on reading the fetched page). — [IMAS 05.10](https://www.mineactionstandards.org/standards/05-10/)
- Humanitarian UAV Code of Conduct (UAViators, 2014, with data-protection guidelines added in 2015): operators should respect humanitarian principles, put safety first, be conflict-sensitive, and "collect, use and store data responsibly". Recommended practice includes informed consent where possible, a data management plan, and blurring people in images (snippet-level, via SciDev.Net). — [SciDev.Net, "Do no harm: a code to guide use of humanitarian drones"](https://www.scidev.net/global/supported-content/do-no-harm-a-code-to-guide-use-of-humanitarian-drones/)
- ICRC/Brussels Privacy Hub guidance (Handbook on Data Protection in Humanitarian Action, chapter on drones) says organisations should protect drone-collected data "even if the individuals recorded in them are not immediately identifiable". It also notes that cross-border transfers need a legal basis and that outsourcing raises controller/processor issues (snippet-level). — [Cambridge Core chapter "Drones/UAVs and remote sensing"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/20F482D33B4158590D7D8810C17403DE)
- In a 2023 workshop report, data sensitivity is described as "a hot topic" for mine action in conflict areas (snippet-level). — [Global Protection Cluster, Amman data sharing workshop report](https://globalprotectioncluster.org:443/sites/default/files/2023-12/amman_data_sharing_workshop_report.pdf)

### Inferences
- Wording for MineGuard outputs that follows IMAS 07.11: "suspected anomaly / suspected hazard (simulated, indirect evidence only)". Avoid "mine detected", "confirmed", "clear" or "safe". Any area with no detections should be labelled "no suspected anomaly found in simulation". It must not be called "low risk" or "safe", because §4.2 forbids decisions based on lack of evidence.
- A false negative from a mapping tool is the costliest error: if users read a blank map as "no hazard", it could lead to inappropriate release of land. That is why IMAS requires QA/QC and a specified depth before land counts as cleared. Over-claiming PD is a safety risk, not just a scientific one.
- Even simulated maps should be watermarked "SIMULATION – NOT FOR OPERATIONAL USE", carry no real coordinates of real contaminated areas, and keep any real geodata under the owner's (NMAA's) policy. IMAS 05.10's ownership and security clauses support this, even though it has no location-specific rule.

- IMAS 07.11 has no formal definition of "confidence" (it uses the term qualitatively).

## 3. How have researchers used simulation/synthetic data for landmine detection, and what credibility pitfalls exist?

### Takeaway
Synthetic data is widely used: gprMax FDTD simulations for GPR B-scans, procedural or rendered thermal and RGB imagery for drones, and cut-and-paste compositing. Reported evidence keeps finding a domain gap. Classifiers trained only on simulation do poorly on real data unless the simulator is calibrated against real sensor and soil measurements, real noise is injected, real data is mixed in, and testing uses held-out real data, ideally from a different site. Real multispectral drone datasets for landmines (for example AMLID) have only recently appeared and carry their own caveats about generalisation.

### Cited Findings
- gprMax-based landmine pipeline: the GPRlearn project generates gprMax B-scans to train a CNN for landmine detection. Its stated next step is testing on real UAV-collected GPR data, and no validation results were found. — [GPRlearn (GitHub)](https://github.com/will-rice/GPRlearn)
- A review cited in an MDPI Sensors paper reports that classifiers trained only on simulated GPR data gave poor results, because the measurement system is hard to model realistically (snippet-level). — [MDPI Sensors 24(3):836](https://mdpi.com/1424-8220/24/3/836)
- Hybrid training: a Faster-RCNN buried-object study used about 100 real B-scans plus 50 gprMax simulations, adding noise estimated from the real data to the simulated images (snippet-level). — [arXiv:1803.08414](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1803.08414)
- Simulator calibration: an Edinburgh thesis fitted unknown GPR antenna parameters by minimising the mismatch between simulated and measured responses, then trained models on the resulting synthetic data (snippet-level). — [Edinburgh Research Archive 1842/40467](https://era.ed.ac.uk/handle/1842/40467); sim-to-real transfer for far-field GPR — [arXiv:2610.19265 (FFDT)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.19265)
- A 2026 arXiv paper reports that synthetic landmine generation has mostly been 2D cut-and-paste of templates onto real backgrounds, which cannot reproduce how surroundings shape a mine's appearance. It notes that thermal detection of the PFM-1 in vegetation has severely limited training data (snippet-level). — [arXiv:2608.09691](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.09691)
- Synthetic thermal drone imagery (2026): synthetic data inflated structural diversity, which created a measurable feature-space gap from real LWIR. The authors report that matching morphological diversity alone is insufficient to close the thermal gap, and that adding visible-light data moved the distribution further away (snippet-level; target is drones-as-objects, not mines). — [arXiv:2608.17799](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.17799)
- Procedural synthetic thermal aerial data (2025): thermal-trained detectors outperformed visible-trained ones, and replicating aerial viewing angles mattered (snippet-level). — [arXiv:2507.06797](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.06797v1)
- AMLID is described as the first open-source dataset combining RGB and LWIR imagery for UAS-based landmine detection. Its authors caution that models may need fine-tuning or domain adaptation for regions with different terrain, vegetation or soil (snippet-level). — [arXiv:2512.18738 (AMLID)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.18738)
- A drone flyby dataset study with surface-mine scale models found that thermal images do not help when vegetation obstruction is heavy (snippet-level). — [arXiv:2410.19807](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.19807)
- The gprMax developers argue the simulator can supply realistic training data where labelled data is scarce, naming landmine detection as an application. This is a position claim, not a demonstrated field result (snippet-level). — [arXiv:2410.14386](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.14386)

### Inferences
- Main pitfalls: (1) a sim-only train/test split, where test data comes from the same generator and so overstates real performance; (2) unrealistic clutter, such as missing metal fragments, rocks, roots, soil moisture variation and vegetation; (3) target leakage, where a target is easier to separate in simulation than it would be physically, for example by a constant thermal contrast; (4) using scale models or surface-only targets and implying the result applies to buried mines; (5) reporting PD without FAR, or without uncertainty.
- For thermal, mine visibility depends on the time of day and the diurnal heating cycle, and on soil moisture. A credible simulator should vary these and report performance per condition, not one aggregate number. That this matters is supported by the vegetation finding above. The specific diurnal physics is general knowledge, not taken from these sources.

## 4. What would make a simulation-only project credible and useful?

### Takeaway
Combining IMAS principles with the simulation literature gives a checklist. Label every output as a simulated "suspected anomaly". Pre-register a sealed test set built like a test lane, with realistic clutter and varied conditions. Use physics-based or measurement-calibrated sensor models where possible. Report PD and FAR (per area) with exact binomial or Poisson confidence intervals and a ROC curve. Disclose the domain gap explicitly and report negative or null results honestly. Make no operational claims.

### Cited Findings
- T&E should be independent, statistically sufficient ("not arisen from chance"), carefully designed with a precise aim, and reported in a standard format. Trials exist to find problems, not only to confirm success. — [IMAS 03.40](https://www.mineactionstandards.org/standards/03-40/)
- Decisions must rest on evidence, not its absence. Indirect evidence yields only an SHA. — [IMAS 07.11](https://www.mineactionstandards.org/standards/07-11/)
- Clearance needs removal to a specified depth, plus QA/QC that is "transparent and auditable". Simulation cannot provide this. — [IMAS 09.10](https://www.mineactionstandards.org/standards/09-10/)
- With small target counts, even 100% detection gives limited confidence, so report binomial bounds (snippet-level). — [GPR 2nd ed. §12.3](https://www.globalspec.com/reference/29024/203279/12-3-performance-and-test-assessment); one-sided Clopper-Pearson bounds against a requirement — [Sci. Rep. 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1038/s41598-021-03755-1)
- Simulation calibrated against measurements, with real noise injected, transfers better than raw simulation (snippet-level). — [Edinburgh thesis](https://era.ed.ac.uk/handle/1842/40467); [arXiv:1803.08414](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1803.08414); [MDPI Sensors 24(3):836](https://mdpi.com/1424-8220/24/3/836)
- Scene context and background realism matter more than target shape variety alone (snippet-level). — [arXiv:2608.09691](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.09691); [arXiv:2608.17799](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.17799)
- Even real-data models need adaptation for new terrain, vegetation and soil (snippet-level). — [AMLID arXiv:2512.18738](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.18738)
- Responsible data handling: a data management plan, consent where applicable, and protection of imagery even when no one is identifiable. — [SciDev.Net on Humanitarian UAV Code](https://www.scidev.net/global/supported-content/do-no-harm-a-code-to-guide-use-of-humanitarian-drones/); [Cambridge Core drones chapter](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/20F482D33B4158590D7D8810C17403DE); data owned by the NMAA and shared only with authorised parties — [IMAS 05.10](https://www.mineactionstandards.org/standards/05-10/)

### Inferences
Suggested credibility checklist for MineGuard (synthesised from the above, not a quoted standard):
1. **Labelling:** every figure, map, CSV and app screen says "SIMULATION – suspected anomaly only – not for operational use". Never write "mine-free", "safe" or "cleared".
2. **Sealed test set / virtual test lane:** fix the seed, target list, depths, clutter and conditions before tuning; hash the set and commit it; score it once; record every re-run.
3. **Realistic clutter and conditions:** include metal fragments, rocks, roots, vegetation cover, soil moisture and type, and thermal timing. Report results per condition stratum.
4. **Physics-based sensor models:** for example gprMax for GPR or a heat-diffusion soil model for thermal, with parameters taken from cited literature. State clearly that these are uncalibrated against real sensors if that is the case.
5. **Metrics:** PD with a 95% Clopper-Pearson interval; FAR per m² or hectare with an interval; ROC or PD-vs-FAR curves; number of targets and area stated. If needed, add the rule-of-three bound for zero misses.
6. **Out-of-distribution test:** hold out a whole simulated "site" (different soil, clutter density or terrain), not just random tiles, to estimate how robust results are to the domain gap.
7. **Honest negative results:** report the conditions where PD falls or FAR rises (dense vegetation, wet soil, deep or minimum-metal targets), failed approaches, and the fact that no real-world validation was done. IMAS 03.40's point that trials are for finding problems supports this.
8. **Data ethics:** use no real minefield coordinates. If any real geodata is ever used, follow the owner's (NMAA's) sharing and security policy and keep it out of public repos.
9. **Scope claim:** describe MineGuard as at most a concept or technology-demonstrator simulation (IMAS 03.40 category). Any real use would need independent T&E under IMAS T&EPs and NMAA accreditation.

- I found no source on how to report negative results specifically in demining ML. General ML reproducibility literature was not searched.
